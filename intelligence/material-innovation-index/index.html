The Material Innovation Index

Executive Summary

Specification is where circularity gets decided

Every material a designer specifies carries a resource history and an end-of-life question attached to it. The built environment consumes close to half of all materials extracted globally each year, and most of that volume still moves through a one-way, take-make-waste path — mined, manufactured, installed, and eventually landfilled. That arithmetic is now colliding with tightening carbon codes, client sustainability commitments, and a maturing supply of genuinely usable alternatives.

What has changed in the past few years is not the ambition — designers have wanted lower-impact materials for a long time — but the availability of options that perform, scale, and can actually be bought. Mycelium composites, recycled-content textiles, mass timber and carbon-sequestering concretes have moved from trade-show novelty to line items with real market size and real manufacturers behind them.

This index ranks eight materials and material categories that FOIS Intelligence judges most relevant to interior specification today, based on published research, market data and materials science literature rather than a proprietary FOIS survey. It is intended as a starting point for further supplier due diligence, not a substitute for it.

The Index

Eight materials worth specifying attention

01 — BIO-MATERIAL

Mycelium Insulation & Biofabricated Panels

Grown from fungal root structures rather than extracted or synthesised, mycelium composites are being engineered into insulation panels, acoustic tiles and rigid boards. The category is forecast to reach $2.55 billion by 2033, with insulation panels already the single largest product segment.

02 — BIO-MATERIAL

Mycelium (Mushroom) Leather

Biofabricated leather alternatives grown from mycelium in a matter of days are being adopted for upholstery, wall panels and accessories as a lower-impact substitute for animal hide and plastic-based leather, with fashion houses providing an early proving ground for durability and finish.

03 — RECYCLED COMPOSITE

Recycled Ocean-Bound Plastic Textiles

Fibres reclaimed from ocean and coastal plastic waste are increasingly spun into upholstery and drapery textiles. The global recycled ocean plastics market, valued near $1.8 billion, is projected to reach $2.9 billion by 2030, with soft-goods applications already its largest use case.

04 — LOW-CARBON STRUCTURE

Cross-Laminated Timber & Mass Timber

Engineered timber panels are displacing steel and concrete in structure and exposed interior surfaces, valued for sequestered carbon, acoustic warmth and speed of assembly. The CLT market is projected to grow from roughly $2.0 billion in 2026 to $5.7 billion by 2033.

05 — LOW-CARBON STRUCTURE

Biochar-Enhanced, Carbon-Negative Concrete

Blending biochar — a stable, carbon-rich char produced from biomass — into concrete as a partial cement replacement can sequester CO2 within the material itself while maintaining structural performance, offering a lower-embodied-carbon base for floors and cast-in-place finishes.

06 — SMART / RESPONSIVE

Thermochromic & Self-Healing Coatings

Materials science research is advancing coatings and composites that shift colour with temperature change or repair minor surface damage autonomously — pointing toward worktops, wall finishes and textiles that respond to use and extend their own service life.

07 — RECYCLED COMPOSITE

Recycled Composite Surfacing

Terrazzo-style and solid surfacing products manufactured from post-consumer and post-industrial waste streams — glass, plastics, textile offcuts — are gaining specification as a circular alternative to virgin stone and laminate worktops and cladding.

08 — LOW-CARBON STRUCTURE

Low-Carbon Cement Alternatives

Beyond biochar, supplementary cementitious materials and novel binder chemistries are being commercialised to cut the embodied carbon of poured and precast interior elements, tracked by analysts as a distinct and fast-growing construction materials category.

Methodology

How this index was built

This index was compiled by the FOIS Intelligence desk through review of published market research, peer-reviewed materials science literature and reporting from sustainability bodies — it does not draw on a proprietary FOIS survey or first-party lab testing. Materials were selected and ranked for their relevance to interior specification, their current commercial maturity, and the strength of publicly available third-party data, then reviewed by the FOIS Intelligence editorial team. Ranking reflects present-day relevance to practising designers rather than a single composite performance score, and figures throughout should be attributed to the third parties cited in Sources, not treated as independently verified by FOIS.
