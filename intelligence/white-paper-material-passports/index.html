Material Passports: A Practitioner's Primer

What material passports actually are, why they matter for circularity and deconstruction, and how a working interior designer can start using one on their next project.

Introduction

Waste is material without an identity

A material passport is a structured record — attached to a product, a component or a whole building — that documents what something is made of, where it came from, what condition it's in, and how it can be removed and reused at the end of its current life. Think of it as a product's CV: composition, origin, installation method, and disassembly instructions, kept somewhere it can actually be found again in ten or twenty years.

The idea has existed in EU circular-economy policy circles for close to a decade, most notably through the Buildings as Material Banks (BAMB) research project, and is now migrating from academic pilots into commercial platforms and, gradually, into interior design practice. It remains, for most practitioners, more talked about than used — which is exactly why this primer exists.

"Waste is material without an identity." — Thomas Rau, architect and circular-economy advocate, on the core problem material passports are built to solve.

For interior designers specifically, material passports matter because interiors churn far faster than the buildings that hold them — a fit-out is often stripped and replaced well within a building's structural lifespan. Without a record of what's actually in a wall, a floor or a ceiling system, almost everything removed during that churn is downcycled or landfilled rather than recovered.

How to Start

A practitioner's checklist

01

Audit What You Already Have

Before adopting any new tool, inventory the materials and products already specified in a current or recent project — composition, supplier, installation method and condition — even in a simple spreadsheet.

02

Choose a Format, Don't Invent One

Align with an established schema rather than building a bespoke system from scratch — BAMB-derived templates, dedicated platforms such as Madaster, or frameworks like the EU's Level(s) all give you a starting structure and a shared language with suppliers.

03

Capture Data at Specification, Not After

Record material data sheets, environmental product declarations (EPDs) and supplier information at the moment a product is chosen — retrofitting this information after installation is far harder and far less reliable.

04

Design for Disassembly

Specify mechanical fixings, reversible joints and accessible layers wherever budget and code allow — a passport is only useful if what it describes can actually be taken apart and recovered later.

05

Attach the Record to Something Durable

Link passport data to a BIM model, a shared digital register, or even a well-labelled PDF stored with the building's operations documents — so it survives staff turnover and outlives the original project team.

06

Ask Suppliers for the Data

Make EPDs and material composition data a standard procurement request, not a special one. Consistent demand from designers is what pushes manufacturers to provide this information as a matter of course.

Methodology

How this primer was built

This primer synthesises published research on material passports and circular construction — including the EU-funded Buildings as Material Banks (BAMB) project, Ellen MacArthur Foundation circularity research, European Commission waste statistics and industry commentary — reviewed by the FOIS Intelligence editorial team. It does not report original FOIS data collection or a survey of practitioners; every figure and claim is attributed to its original source in the Sources section below.
