Why Circularity Can't Stay Optional

We keep talking about circular design as a frontier — a nice-to-have for the ambitious client. I don't think we can afford to keep talking about it that way.

Sept 2026Published
7 MinReading Time
OpinionFormat
EditorialDesk

I have sat through enough design review meetings to know how the circularity conversation usually goes. It arrives near the end of the agenda, after the finishes have been chosen and the budget has been set, framed as a bonus: "if we have room, let's source some reclaimed material" or "it would be nice to specify for disassembly." It is treated as a virtue, not a requirement — something a project can have, the way it can have a green wall, rather than something it must have, the way it must have a fire rating.

I want to make the case, plainly, that this has to change. Not because circularity is fashionable — it is, but that is the least interesting reason — but because the numbers describing how we currently build and furnish interiors are no longer numbers we can responsibly ignore.

The Cost of Convenience

We are extracting half the planet's materials to build things we then throw away

Here is the fact that reframed this issue for me: the built environment consumes almost half of all materials extracted globally each year. Not half of industrial materials, or half of construction inputs specifically — almost half of everything mined, quarried, harvested, and pumped out of the earth in a twelve-month period goes toward the buildings and interiors we occupy. And on current trajectory, the world will build the material equivalent of an entire city the size of Paris every single week between now and 2060.

Meanwhile, the European Union's own waste statistics attribute more than a third of all waste generated across the bloc — over 37% — to construction activity alone. That is not a niche waste stream. It is the largest single category of waste the EU produces, larger than municipal waste, larger than manufacturing waste, larger than anything else on the ledger.

And of the construction and demolition material that does get collected for "recycling," most of it is not entering a genuine circular loop. Industry analysis puts the share of construction and demolition waste that is meaningfully recycled or reused — as opposed to crushed for low-grade backfill — at somewhere between 20 and 30 percent globally. We have built an entire recycling narrative around a process that, for the most part, downcycles rather than truly circulates.

We didn't design a linear system by accident. We designed it by never being asked to design anything else.

What "Optional" Actually Costs Us

The climate math is not ambiguous

I understand the counterargument. Circular specification takes longer, costs more up front, complicates procurement, and asks designers to think several building lifecycles ahead in a profession that is usually paid to think about the next eighteen months. Those are real constraints, and I don't want to wave them away with a slogan.

But set against those constraints is a number I find hard to argue past: modelling from the Ellen MacArthur Foundation suggests that applying circular economy principles to how we source and reuse building materials — steel, aluminium, cement, plastic — could cut global CO2 emissions from building materials by 38% by 2050. That is not a marginal efficiency gain. That is the difference between a construction sector that helps meet a climate target and one that quietly undermines it, regardless of how efficient the HVAC system upstairs is.

Every time we specify virgin material where reclaimed or designed-for-reuse material was a real option, we are not making a neutral aesthetic choice. We are making a climate choice, and pretending otherwise is the convenient fiction the whole industry has been allowed to keep telling itself.

What Changes If We Take This Seriously

Circularity as baseline, not bonus

Taking this seriously does not mean every project becomes a reclaimed-timber showcase. It means something more structural: material passports and disassembly documentation become as standard as a lighting plan. Specification defaults to reused or design-for-disassembly options, with virgin material as the exception that requires justification, not the other way around. Clients ask their designers what happens to a space's materials at end of life in the same breath they ask about budget and timeline. And schools training the next generation of interior designers — including our own — teach deconstruction and material lifecycle thinking as a core competency, not an elective module for the environmentally inclined.

None of this requires waiting for regulation to force our hand, though regulation focused on construction and demolition waste is clearly coming and clearly overdue. It requires the industry — practitioners, educators, and platforms like this one — to stop treating circularity as a value-add and start treating it as a baseline professional standard, the same way we already treat structural safety or accessibility.

I don't think this is a radical position. I think it is closer to catching up with numbers we have already had access to for years, and simply chose to file under "someday."

"Someday" is not a material strategy. It is the absence of one, dressed up to sound patient.
