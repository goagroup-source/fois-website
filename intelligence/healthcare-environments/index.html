Healthcare Environments After the Pandemic

The infection data is improving. The renovation pipeline is accelerating. And the evidence base for what a healing environment actually does to a patient's body is now hard to ignore in a design brief.

Sept 2026Published
10 MinReading Time
HealthcareSector

Executive Summary

Evidence-based design has moved from research to requirement

Five years on from the acute phase of the pandemic, healthcare design is being driven less by emergency retrofit and more by an accumulating, quantifiable evidence base. National infection surveillance data shows measurable long-term improvement, even as certain infection categories plateau — and design is increasingly named, alongside clinical protocol, as part of why.

At the same time, the healthcare construction pipeline has shifted decisively toward renovation over new build, reflecting a sector prioritising flexibility and rapid reconfiguration over speculative new capacity. Outpatient migration continues, but 2025 data shows inpatient hospital projects reclaiming share — a signal that acute-care environments themselves are being substantially reworked, not just supplemented.

Underneath the project data sits a decades-old but newly urgent research literature: single-patient rooms, access to daylight and nature, and acoustic control are no longer framed as comfort features. They are framed, increasingly, as clinical infrastructure with measurable outcomes.

Analysis

What the evidence base actually supports

Single-patient rooms and infection control. A growing body of systematic review evidence links single-patient room design in intensive and acute care to reduced nosocomial infection incidence, reinforcing decades of evidence-based design advocacy — most influentially Roger Ulrich's foundational research linking the physical environment to measurable clinical outcomes.

Access to nature and daylight. Recent rapid reviews of biophilic design in hospital settings continue to find associations between nature-integrated environments and improved patient stress, mood, and reported recovery experience, building on Ulrich's original finding that surgical patients with a view of nature had measurably shorter, less complicated recoveries than those without one.

Acoustic environment as a clinical variable. Hospital ward noise is increasingly studied as a physiological stressor, not merely a comfort complaint, with acoustic-first material and layout decisions now appearing in the same design conversations as infection control and staff workflow.

Renovation over new build, by necessity and by design philosophy. With two-thirds of 2025 healthcare projects being renovations, the sector is favouring adaptability and speed over speculative new capacity — a pattern reinforced by construction cost escalation and labour constraints named by nearly one in five A/E/C firms as a top-three obstacle.

The healthcare interior is being asked to do something few other building types are: prove, in infection rates and recovery times, that the design decision was clinically correct.

Methodology

How this report was built

FOIS Intelligence has not conducted a primary clinical or practitioner survey for this report. The statistics and analysis above synthesise published federal surveillance data (CDC), industry survey findings (Healthcare Design Magazine's annual A/E/C survey), and peer-reviewed evidence-based design literature, each credited individually in the Sources section below rather than presented as FOIS's own fieldwork.

Where a study finding is described qualitatively (for example, "associated with" or "linked to"), that phrasing is intentional and reflects the underlying research's own framing of correlation versus causation.
